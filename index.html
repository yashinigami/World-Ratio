AFTER THE BASICS

A few ways people approach investing

These are educational frameworks to help explain how investors think. They are not instructions or guaranteed outcomes.

IMPORTANT: This section is for information and learning only. It is not actual financial or investment advice, nor a recommendation to buy or sell any security. Suitability depends on individual goals, circumstances, time horizon and ability to bear losses.
01↗

Buy and hold

Investors buy an asset with a long-term view and hold through shorter market swings, usually because they believe its value can grow over time.

Real-life example: A learner studies a listed company, its sales, debt, competition and profits. They track its annual reports over several years instead of reacting to every daily price move, while reassessing if the business changes.

Keep in mind: Long holding periods do not remove the risk of loss. The business and your reasons for owning it still matter.

02◌

Rupee-cost averaging

A fixed amount is invested at regular intervals. When prices are lower, the same amount buys more units; when prices are higher, it buys fewer.

Real-life example: If a person invests ₹1,000 monthly, a unit price of ₹100 buys 10 units, while a price of ₹80 buys 12.5 units. The same amount buys fewer units when the price rises. This changes the average purchase cost but does not guarantee a positive return.

Keep in mind: It can support disciplined investing, but it does not guarantee a profit or protect against falling markets.

03✿

Diversification

Money is spread across different companies, sectors or asset types so the outcome does not depend on just one holding.

Real-life example: A learner compares a hypothetical mix of shares from unrelated industries, bonds and cash. A problem in one company may have less influence than it would in a portfolio holding only that company and its close competitors.

Keep in mind: Diversification can reduce concentration risk, but it cannot eliminate market risk.

04⌕

Value investing

Investors look for securities they believe are priced below a reasonable estimate of their underlying worth, based on research and assumptions.

Real-life example: Two shares both trade at ₹100. One company has steady cash flow and manageable debt; the other has falling sales and heavy borrowing. A learner studies the businesses and their financial statements rather than assuming the same share price means the same value.

Keep in mind: An apparently cheap stock may be cheap for a reason. Valuation is uncertain and requires careful analysis.

05◎

Growth investing

What it means: Focusing on businesses whose sales, earnings or cash flows are expected to grow faster than average. Investors may accept a higher current valuation because they expect future performance to justify it.

In simple terms: You are thinking about what a business could become, not just what it earns today. But expectations can be too optimistic, and a promising company can still be overpriced.

Real-life example: A learner studies a software company expanding into new markets. They look beyond revenue growth to customer retention, competition, costs and whether the company is turning growth into cash.

Risks: Growth can slow, competition can increase and high valuations can fall sharply when expectations change.

06⌘

Index investing

What it means: Using a fund designed to track a published market index instead of selecting individual shares. The fund aims to follow the index performance, before fees and tracking differences.

In simple terms: Rather than trying to guess which one company will do best, this approach gives exposure to a basket of companies represented by an index.

Real-life example: A NIFTY 50 index fund aims to track a selected group of large, liquid Indian companies. A learner can examine its costs, tracking difference and index rules to understand its exposure.

Risks: An index fund still falls when the market segment it tracks falls. Different indices carry different concentrations and risks.

07◷

Asset allocation and rebalancing

What it means: Asset allocation is how a portfolio is divided among groups such as shares, bonds and cash. Rebalancing means reviewing and adjusting those proportions when market movements cause them to drift.

In simple terms: If shares rise much faster than bonds, shares may become a much larger part of the portfolio than originally planned. Rebalancing is a way of checking whether that changed mix still matches the intended risk level.

Real-life example: A hypothetical portfolio starts with 50% shares and 50% bonds. After a strong equity period it becomes 65% shares and 35% bonds. A learner would consider the original plan, transaction costs and possible taxes before any adjustment.

Risks: There is no universally correct mix. Rebalancing can create costs and may involve selling assets that have recently risen.

08▤

Income investing

What it means: Focusing on investments that may generate cash income, such as dividends from shares or interest from bonds. The amount and reliability depend on the issuer and the instrument terms.

In simple terms: Some investors pay attention to the cash an investment might distribute, not only whether its market price increases. But dividends can be reduced and bond issuers can miss payments.

Real-life example: A learner compares two bonds by looking at coupon payments, maturity dates, credit quality and market prices. They also ask whether the quoted yield compensates for missed-payment and price risks.

Risks: A high yield can signal higher risk. Income is not guaranteed, and the investment market value can fall.

Learning material only — not personal investment advice or a recommendation to buy or sell any security.

LET'S CLEAR THE AIR

Common market misconceptions

Things that sound convincing in a group chat, but need a little more context.

01 / VALUATION

“A low P/E means a stock is undervalued.”

Not necessarily. A low P/E can reflect weak growth expectations, falling profits, high business risk or temporarily high earnings. Compare companies in context and examine the quality and sustainability of earnings.

02 / DIVIDENDS

“A high dividend yield is always better.”

Not always. Yield can look high because the share price has fallen sharply. Dividends can also be reduced or stopped. Check cash flow, payout sustainability, debt and why the price has moved.

03 / PRICE

“A ₹50 share is cheaper than a ₹2,000 share.”

Price alone tells you little. The number of shares, earnings, assets and growth prospects matter. Valuation ratios and market capitalisation give more context than comparing share prices in isolation.

04 / RISK

“A famous, large company is a safe investment.”

Size is no safety guarantee. Established companies can face disruption, debt stress, poor decisions and falling valuations. Every equity investment carries risk, including loss of capital.

05 / DIVERSIFICATION

“Owning many stocks automatically means I'm diversified.”

Not if they behave alike. Many companies in one sector can still create concentration. Consider sector, asset-class, geography and underlying risk exposures—not just the number of holdings.

06 / TECHNICALS

“An RSI below 30 means a share must bounce.”

It is not a promise. RSI below 30 is commonly called oversold, but a price can remain weak or fall further. Indicators help interpret price behaviour; they are not guarantees or standalone buy signals.

07 / TIMING

“You only make money when prices go up.”

It depends on the instrument and strategy. Investors may receive dividends or bond income, and some strategies can benefit from falling prices. Each approach has distinct risks, costs and rules.

08 / RETURNS

“Past returns tell me what I'll earn next year.”

Past performance is not a forecast. Market conditions, valuations, business results and rates change. Historical returns offer context, not a promise of future performance.

About the project

← Home

The financial history of the world, made explorable.

"World Ratio (Finance)" is an independent financial-history exploration project developed by an MSc Finance student based in India.

It was created to make learning about financial history easier, more accessible and more engaging by connecting financial events with the places, institutions and people they have affected.

From major financial crises and government policies to local economic developments, World Ratio aims to help people understand how financial decisions and events have shaped the world.

RESEARCH PRINCIPLES

Evidence before volume.

Events are stored as structured records with source links. The initial dataset is intentionally small. An empty location or year means the current sample contains no verified record — it does not mean nothing happened there.

  • Prefer official reports, regulators, central banks and primary records.
  • Use reputable journalism and academic research for context.
  • Distinguish facts, estimates, interpretation and causal claims.
  • Never invent a financial event to fill a map.
